# Benchmarking LLM-Driven Program Search: Performance, Cost, Statistics, Fidelity, and Overfitting

Scope note: survey of benchmarking methodology for stochastic search, adapted to expensive LLM-driven code evolution (AlphaEvolve-style systems such as OpenEvolve 0.4.0). Notes compiled 2026-11-06. Every claim carries a URL. Facts taken from search snippets or secondary summaries rather than the primary text are flagged. Recommendations for an AFAR-style setting (about 42 C++ candidates per generation, about 5 CPU-h per candidate, 14 traces, geomean IPC speedup over LRU, 48 h campaigns, few seeds) appear under each question's "Inferences" and are labelled as inferences.

## Q1. How should stochastic search algorithms be compared? (fixed-budget vs fixed-target, anytime curves, ERT/ECDF, number of runs, statistical tests, the 2–5-run regime)

### Takeaway
The optimization-benchmarking literature treats an optimizer's result as a distribution over runs and over time, not as a single number. Report the best-so-far trajectory against a cost axis. Summarize it with fixed-target measures (time-to-target, ERT, ECDFs) and/or an anytime area measure (area under the ECDF or convergence curve). Compare configurations with effect sizes and interval estimates (bootstrap or Bayesian) rather than p-values alone. With 2–5 runs per configuration, conventional significance is close to unreachable: an exact Mann–Whitney test cannot reach p < 0.05 with 3 vs 3 runs. Designs must instead borrow strength from structure (paired seeds, per-instance stratification, sequential allocation of extra runs) and report uncertainty honestly.

### Cited Findings

**Fixed-budget vs fixed-target, and anytime performance**
- Bartz-Beielstein et al. (17 authors, including C. Doerr, López-Ibáñez, Kerschke, La Cava, Wagner, Weise) say a fixed-budget (vertical cut) measure "comes with the benefit that its results are well-defined." Fixed-target (horizontal cut) results are easier to interpret, but users "need to define how they treat such non-successful runs." For anytime behaviour, "the performance does not correspond to a singular point, but instead to an entire curve," and the right view "depends on the application at hand" — [Bartz-Beielstein et al. 2020, "Benchmarking in Optimization: Best Practice and Open Issues"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03488)
- COCO (Hansen, Auger, Brockhoff, Tušar, Tušar) prefers fixed-target: "the fixed-target approach is superior to the fixed-budget approach since it gives quantitative and interpretable results." It holds that runtime is "the only available measure with a generic, meaningful, and quantitative interpretation." Fixed-target measures are invariant under order-preserving transformations of the objective, provided the targets are transformed too — [COCO: Performance Assessment, arXiv:1605.03560](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.03560)
- COCO defines runtime as the evaluation count at which the quality indicator first drops below the target. Targets are a reference value plus a precision offset. Targets can also be "runlength-based," derived from a reference algorithm's performance at chosen budgets — [arXiv:1605.03560](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.03560)
- Anytime assessment: Hansen, Auger, Brockhoff and Tušar, "Anytime performance assessment in blackbox optimization benchmarking," IEEE TEVC 26(6):1293–1305, 2022 (citation found via a reference list; full text not read) — [cited in López-Ibáñez et al. EAF paper, arXiv:2404.02031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02031)
- The area under the target-based ECDF measures anytime performance, and COCO minimizes the area *over* the ECDF curve. The AUC of the target-based ECDF is equivalent to the AUC of the empirical attainment function (EAF). The "average area over the convergence curves is a simpler-to-calculate, but equivalent" anytime measure. The EAF costs more to compute and store and has been added to IOHanalyzer (from search snippets of the paper) — [Using the Empirical Attainment Function for Analyzing Single-objective Black-box Optimization Algorithms, arXiv:2404.02031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02031)
- LLaMEA (LLM-driven metaheuristic generation) uses AOCC, the normalized area over the convergence curve, as the fitness fed back to the LLM. It is "equivalent to the area under the so-called ECDF curve with infinite targets between the chosen bounds." Runs with fatal errors get AOCC = 0 (from search snippets; versions conflict on 3 vs 5 runs per instance) — [LLaMEA, arXiv:2405.20132](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20132); AOCC is also used in [In-the-loop HPO for LLM-based automated design of heuristics, arXiv:2410.16309](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16309)

**ERT, ECDFs and success rates (COCO, IOHprofiler)**
- Expected runtime of a restarted algorithm: E(RT) = E(RT^s) + ((1 − p_s)/p_s)·E(RT^us). In practice aRT = total evaluations over all trials, successful and unsuccessful, divided by the number of successful trials — [arXiv:1605.03560](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.03560)
- COCO simulates restarts by bootstrap: it draws trials with replacement until one succeeds, repeating 100–1,000 times. Simulated restarts may be too pessimistic beyond the maximal budget when the success probability is below 1/2 — [arXiv:1605.03560](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.03560)
- The runtime ECDF shows the fraction of (problem, target) pairs solved within a budget. COCO aggregates over targets and function groups but "never over dimensions" — [arXiv:1605.03560](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.03560)
- COCO uses about K ≈ 15 independent runs (one per instance) per problem. Success probabilities are comparable only down to about 2/K, and 4/15 successes vs 0/15 is not significant at the 5% level — [arXiv:1605.03560](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.03560)
- IOHprofiler/IOHanalyzer define ERT as assuming independent restarts after any unsuccessful run, which makes ERT infinite if no run succeeds. The fixed-target ECDF is "the fraction of runs which successfully found a solution of quality at least v within a budget of at most t function evaluations." Aggregation over a target set pools (run, target) pairs — [IOHprofiler background page](https://iohprofiler.github.io/Background); [IOHanalyzer paper, arXiv:2007.03953](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03953)
- The IOHanalyzer R package provides `AUC(fun, from, to)` for ECDF objects and a radar plot of the area under the aggregated ECDF (`Plot.FV.ECDF_AUC`) — [IOHanalyzer R reference: AUC](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/IOHanalyzer/html/AUC.html)
- IOHprofiler, the platform for benchmarking iterative optimization heuristics that supports both fixed-budget and fixed-target analysis — [Doerr et al., arXiv:1810.05281](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.05281)

**Reporting practice (Bartz-Beielstein et al.)**
- "Best practice would be to report both" function evaluations and CPU time, and "if generations are reported, FEs should be reported as well." When single evaluations are expensive, the FE count "is a good proxy for clock time," and CPU time should ideally be split into the expensive FE part and algorithm overhead — [arXiv:2007.03488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03488)
- Run exploratory analysis to check distributional assumptions before confirmatory statistics. Medians or geometric means are robust alternatives to the arithmetic mean. The paper criticizes reporting only means ± SD, p-values, or boxplots — [arXiv:2007.03488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03488)
- On fairness, "it is crucial to avoid bad parameter configurations and to properly tune the algorithms under consideration," and "automated tuning is state of the art and highly recommended." Tuned configurations are budget-specific. "Let all candidate algorithms use the same starting points" — [arXiv:2007.03488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03488)
- On reproducibility, fix and store random seeds, use containers, and compare against current implementations — [arXiv:2007.03488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03488). Broader treatment: [López-Ibáñez, Branke & Paquete, "Reproducibility in Evolutionary Computation," ACM TELO 2021, arXiv:2102.03380](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03380)

**GP benchmarking critiques**
- McDermott et al. (GECCO 2012, pp. 791–798) argue that GP benchmarking lacks rigor and propose a path toward a standard benchmark set — [McDermott et al., "Genetic programming needs better benchmarks," DOI 10.1145/2330163.2330273](https://doi.org/10.1145/2330163.2330273); [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/71321)
- White et al. (GPEM 14:3–29, 2013) ran a community survey (open 9–31 July 2012) after the GECCO debate. They found consensus that problem selection and experimental rigor need to improve, and proposed blacklisting several commonly used problems and replacing them (details from search snippets; the 79-response count comes from a secondary aggregator and is unverified) — [White et al., "Better GP benchmarks: community survey results and proposals," DOI 10.1007/s10710-012-9177-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10710-012-9177-2)

**How many runs / seeds**
- Colas, Sigaud and Oudeyer report that every deep-RL paper they reviewed used ≤ 5 seeds. Splitting 10 runs of one algorithm into two groups of 5 can yield curves that look like different algorithms. The paper gives power-analysis guidance for Welch's t-test and bootstrap tests, plus code — [Colas et al., "How Many Random Seeds?", arXiv:1806.08295](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.08295)
- Agarwal et al. ("Deep RL at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice," NeurIPS 2021) note that 3–10 runs per task are typical and that point estimates from them are unreliable. Tight median CIs need about 50–100 runs, and sample medians are biased. They recommend stratified-bootstrap CIs (percentile CIs work from about 10 runs), the interquartile mean (IQM, a 25% trimmed mean, more efficient and with negligible bias), performance profiles with confidence bands, and "probability of improvement." Many published improvements were only 50–70% likely. Their library is rliable — [Agarwal et al., arXiv:2108.13264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264)
- A 2026 LLM-evolution paper applies exactly this protocol to program search: 10 seeds per cell, stratified bootstrap SEs and 95% CIs (1,000 resamples, "following the rliable protocol"), a permutation test (20,000 shuffles), and time-to-threshold — [Xing et al., "Compute Allocation in Evolutionary Search," arXiv:2605.29268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29268)

**Statistical tests, effect sizes, Bayesian alternatives, multiplicity**
- Arcuri and Briand (ICSE 2011; STVR 2014) found that randomness is often not properly accounted for in randomized-algorithm studies. Their guidelines cover non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U), the Vargha–Delaney Â12 effect size reported alongside p-values, confidence intervals, and multiple-comparison adjustment (Bonferroni) — [ICSE 2011 page](https://2011.icse-conferences.org/content/practical-guide-using-statistical-tests-assess-randomized-algorithms-software-engineering.html); [DOI 10.1145/1985793.1985795](https://doi.org/10.1145/1985793.1985795); recommendation list as summarized in [arXiv:2608.24850](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.24850)
- Vargha–Delaney A = P(X > Y) + 0.5·P(X = Y), the "measure of stochastic superiority." Conventional thresholds of 0.56 / 0.64 / 0.71 mark small / medium / large effects — [Vargha & Delaney 2000, J. Educ. Behav. Stat. 25(2), DOI 10.3102/10769986025002101](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986025002101) (thresholds from prior knowledge of the paper)
- Derrac et al. give the standard EC tutorial on non-parametric testing: Wilcoxon signed-rank for pairwise comparison across problems, and Friedman-type tests with post-hoc procedures such as Holm for multiple algorithms — [Derrac et al. 2011, Swarm & Evol. Comput. 1(1):3–18, DOI 10.1016/j.swevo.2011.02.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.swevo.2011.02.002)
- Multiplicity corrections: Holm's step-down procedure, [Holm 1979, Scand. J. Stat.](https://www.jstor.org/stable/4615733), and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control, [Benjamini & Hochberg 1995, DOI 10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x)
- Bayesian comparisons. Calvo et al. (GECCO 2019) introduced Bayesian performance analysis to EC benchmarking, using a Plackett–Luce model over rankings of 11 EAs on 23 problems — [HAL hal-02179609](https://hal.sorbonne-universite.fr/hal-02179609). Rojas-Delgado, Ceberio, Calvo and Lozano (IEEE TEVC 2022) estimate posteriors such as "probability that algorithm X ranks first," with a Python package — [slides/PDF](https://events.gwdg.de/event/482/attachments/391/582/Bayesian_Performance_Analysis_for_Algorithm_Ranking_Comparison.pdf). Benavoli et al. argue against black-and-white NHST and propose Bayesian signed-rank tests with a region of practical equivalence (ROPE) — [Benavoli et al., JMLR 18(77), 2017, "Time for a change"](https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/16-305.html)
- Best-of-k statistics: the sample maximum is a poor bootstrap target. One LLM-evolution study computes equal-cost comparisons with exact order statistics (averaging over which k seeds are drawn) and switches to subsampling (m = 20 of 40) where the bootstrap is inconsistent for a sample maximum — [Oved et al., arXiv:2610.23977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23977). Expected maximum performance as a function of the number of trials is also the basis of "expected validation performance" reporting — [Dodge et al. 2019, "Show Your Work," arXiv:1909.03004](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.03004)
- Seed variance can make two groups of runs of the *same* algorithm look significantly different — [Henderson et al. 2018, "Deep RL that Matters," arXiv:1709.06560](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06560)

**Small-sample arithmetic (own computation, exact permutation distribution of Mann–Whitney U, no ties)**
- With n runs per arm, the minimum achievable two-sided exact p-value is 2/C(2n, n): n = 2 → 0.333; n = 3 → 0.100; n = 4 → 0.029; n = 5 → 0.0079. The resolution of Â12 is 1/n² (0.11 for n = 3). Computed locally; see Inferences.

### Inferences
- **The 2–5-run regime.** At 3 vs 3 seeds, no rank test can declare significance at α = 0.05. At 4 vs 4 (minimum p = 0.029), a Holm correction across even 2 comparisons pushes the smallest attainable adjusted p above 0.05. The practical consequence is to estimate rather than test with few campaigns: report every seed's curve, a robust centre (median or IQM), Â12 or probability-of-improvement, and bootstrap or Bayesian intervals (Agarwal et al.; Benavoli et al.). Say explicitly that the intervals are wide.
- **Borrow strength from structure.** (a) Stratify by trace: each candidate already yields 14 per-trace speedups. A two-level (seed, then trace) stratified bootstrap of final-best-program speedups, in rliable's "tasks" style, gives more informative intervals. Trace-level variation is not search variance, though, so resample seeds at the outer level. (b) Pair seeds across configurations: OpenEvolve seeds "every component" (default `random_seed: 42`). LLM sampling under vLLM batching may still be non-deterministic (see Q3 gaps). (c) Use sequential allocation: run 1–2 seeds of many lever settings, then add seeds only to the contenders, applying racing logic (see Q4) at campaign level.
- **The headline measure.** Best improvement per unit compute, used alone as a final ratio (best speedup ÷ total CPU+GPU-h), is neither a fixed-budget nor a fixed-target quantity. Under diminishing returns it favours stopping early, and it is not invariant to the cost exchange rate. Report instead, per seed: (1) the best-so-far fitness curve vs cumulative cost; (2) fixed-budget value at the campaign budget; (3) fixed-target cost-to-reach for 2–3 pre-registered speedup targets, with runs that never reach a target handled by ERT; (4) normalized area under the best-so-far curve (an AOCC-style anytime measure). The "per unit compute" idea is captured properly by (3) and (4).
- **Best-of-k.** If the team intends to run k campaigns and keep the best program, the decision-relevant quantity is E[max of k campaigns], estimated via exact order statistics over seeds (Oved et al.; Dodge et al.). It is not the best single run ever observed.

### Gaps
- The Bartz-Beielstein et al. sections on confirmatory testing, power analysis and experimental design (Sections 6.3–7) were beyond the portion fetched. Their specific run-count and test recommendations are not quoted here.
- The full text of Hansen et al. 2022 (anytime assessment, TEVC) was not read. Its exact formulation is cited only via a secondary reference.

## Q2. How do LLM-driven evolution papers account for cost, and how do they normalize comparisons?

### Takeaway
Cost reporting in this literature is inconsistent and often incomplete. Most systems report budgets in generations, proposals or "samples" (ShinkaEvolve's "150 samples," AlphaEvolve's "thousands" vs FunSearch's "millions"). Fewer report dollars and wall time (CodeEvolve; Zhang et al.). Almost none report tokens or GPU-hours. Two 2026 papers argue this biases comparisons. Equal-proposal comparisons can give one strategy up to 1.9× the compute of another and give evolution roughly 6× the budget of an independent-sampling baseline. At equal tokens, evolution beats sampling by ≤ 5% on 12 of 15 task–strategy pairs. FLOP-normalized comparisons collapse much of the apparent difference between model sizes. "Sample efficiency" claims are therefore only meaningful with a stated cost axis and a cost-matched sampling baseline.

### Cited Findings

**Systems and what they report**
- AlphaEvolve (Google DeepMind, 2025) reports that "thousands of LLM samples suffice," contrasted with "millions of LLM samples used" by FunSearch (its Table 1). Evaluations can run "for hours, in parallel, on accelerators," with some problems costing "on the order of 100 compute-hours to evaluate any new solution." Math-heuristic runs got "a fixed time budget (e.g., 1000 seconds)." The LLM ensemble is Gemini 2.0 Flash (throughput) plus Gemini 2.0 Pro (quality). The paper gives no overall accelerator-hour or wall-clock total — [AlphaEvolve, arXiv:2506.13131](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13131)
- FunSearch is cited as using "around 1 million queries" — [EoH, arXiv:2401.02051](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02051). An independent estimate puts FunSearch's cost at "between 800$−1400$" on Google Cloud and 250–500 kWh — [Sim, Renau & Hart, arXiv:2501.11411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11411). Original paper: [Romera-Paredes et al., Nature 2023/24](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06924-6) (paywall redirect; not read directly)
- EoH (ICML 2024) reached bin-packing fitness 0.962 → 0.993 in 20 generations with "2,000 LLM queries" and claims efficiency vs FunSearch's ~1M. The FunSearch heuristic was taken from its paper ("We directly use the heuristic generated by FunSearch") rather than re-run under matched budgets. Ablation variants were matched on number of evaluated heuristics — [EoH, arXiv:2401.02051](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02051)
- Zhang et al. (PPSN 2024) benchmarked EoH, FunSearch, ReEvo, a (1+1)-EPS baseline and standalone LLM sampling on 4 problems, 9 LLMs and 5 independent runs, with a 10,000-evaluation budget for EPS methods and up to 100,000 queries for standalone sampling. Findings: (1+1)-EPS with 500 queries beat standalone LLMs with 10,000 queries in most settings. Runs took about 2–10 days. API cost per run was about $10 (GPT-3.5), $100 (GPT-4) and $200 (Claude 3 Opus). The paper recommends consistent initialization, termination and LLM settings; simple baselines; component analysis; and reporting computational and API costs — [Zhang et al., arXiv:2407.10873](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10873)
- ShinkaEvolve (Sakana, 2025) claims a state-of-the-art circle-packing result "using only 150 samples" (Fig. 5: surpasses AlphaEvolve in < 150 evaluations). Costs are reported only in generations/evaluations (circle packing 150 generations, AIME 75, ALE-Bench 50, MoE 20–30), with no token counts or dollar costs. There is no normalized comparison table, and LLM/model differences are not accounted for — [ShinkaEvolve, arXiv:2509.19349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19349); marketing framing "orders of magnitude more efficiently" — [Sakana blog](https://sakana.ai/shinka-evolve/)
- CodeEvolve (Inter&Co, 2025–26, v6 Aug 2026) reports USD and hours per *best* run. Examples: CirclePackingSquare n=26 cost $6.5 / 6.2 h with Qwen3-Coder-30B vs $34.5 / 6.1 h with Gemini-2.5. Qwen needed about 900 LLM calls to surpass AlphaEvolve on n=26 vs about 400 for Gemini, but ran at "almost 10%" of Gemini's cost. No token counts are given. Compute was "a fixed computational budget of vCPUs and RAM" (numbers unstated) — [CodeEvolve v3, arXiv:2510.14150v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14150v3)
- CodeEvolve's comparison with OpenEvolve/ShinkaEvolve "adopted the same configurations and evaluation budgets (with minor adaptations to suit each framework)." Shared settings: 60 s timeout, 10 islands, Qwen3-Coder-30B only, 3 runs per framework. The budget is never defined in calls, tokens or time. Only Mean and Best are reported; CodeEvolve has the best mean on 6 of 9 problems — [CodeEvolve v6, arXiv:2510.14150v6](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14150v6)
- AlgoTune: 154 expert-contributed tasks. Solutions are checked for validity and timed against reference implementations (SciPy, scikit-learn, CVXPY). The baseline AlgoTuner is "a simple, budgeted loop" achieving 1.72× average speedup. The abstract does not state the budget's units or how speedup is aggregated — [AlgoTune, arXiv:2507.15887](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15887)
- ADRS ("Barbarians at the Gate," Berkeley, Oct 2025) uses an OpenEvolve-based loop on systems problems (multi-region load balancing, MoE inference, LLM-SQL, transaction scheduling). It reports up to 5.0× runtime improvements or 50% cost reductions and argues systems research suits this approach because simulators and real systems give reliable verifiers. The abstract gives no cost/time figures — [ADRS, arXiv:2510.06189](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06189)
- BLADE (GECCO '25 companion) is a benchmark suite for LLM-driven algorithm design on continuous black-box optimization (MA-BBOB, SBOX-COST). It logs LLM token costs, seeds and hyperparameters, and integrates IOHanalyzer/IOHexplainer and human baselines (CMA-ES, DE) — [BLADE, arXiv:2504.20183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20183)

**Normalization debates (2026)**
- Oved, Pony, Naparstek and Barzelay (IBM Research, arXiv Oct 2026) replay 600 logged runs: 5 tasks (PRISM, Cloudcast, transaction scheduling, circle packing, Heilbronn) × 3 strategies (OpenEvolve, EvoX, AdaEvolve) × 40 seeds × 200 iterations, using gpt-5-mini via the ADRS engine. Key numbers:
  - Mean tokens per proposal are 22,985 (OpenEvolve), 26,992 (EvoX) and 33,907 (AdaEvolve); across cells the range is 15.6k–38.6k (a 2.5× spread).
  - An independent sample costs 4.1k–6.0k tokens, about 5.7× cheaper.
  - At equal proposals AdaEvolve gets about 1.5× (up to 1.9×) OpenEvolve's compute.
  - Mean evolution/sampling ratio is 1.09 at equal proposals and 1.07 at equal tokens. Evolution beats sampling by ≤ 5% on 12 of 15 pairs; Heilbronn pairs show 23–28%; Cloudcast/OpenEvolve falls to 0.99.
  - Budgeting by model *calls* removes only about 1/8 of the correction that tokens make.
  - Recommendations: report tokens; price sampling baselines at equal tokens; audit top programs by re-execution; log audit metrics; check whether a task rewards search at all.

  — [Oved et al., "LLM Evolutionary Search Strategies Should be Compared by Tokens," arXiv:2610.23977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23977)
- The same paper finds that on a second backbone (gemini-2.5-flash) per-proposal cost spans 16.8k–65.4k tokens (3.9×) and the evolution margin rises to 1.30. Haiku 4.5 reproduces the task-dependent split, so margins depend on the model as well as the task — [arXiv:2610.23977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23977)
- Xing et al. (May 2026) argue LLM calls are not a fair cross-model cost axis. They charge effective FLOPs per call as 2 × P_active × (prompt − cached + output tokens), following Hoffmann et al. Prefix-cache hit rates were 94–98%; ignoring them over-reports FLOPs by 6–9%. Plotted against effective FLOPs, the capability ordering among capable Qwen3 models "largely collapses" (R² = 0.93–0.94). They note "the dominant convention is to report only the best of an unspecified number of runs," and that per-run budgets span >2 orders of magnitude, from about 150 LLM calls (ShinkaEvolve) to 204,800 candidates (a ThetaEvolve run) — [Xing et al., arXiv:2605.29268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29268)
- Xing et al. also find multi-generation refinement beats best-of-N at equal calls (up to +0.12 normalized fitness at C = 128). The depth/breadth split (generations T × children N) has a task-dependent optimum: an interior ridge on MinMaxDist and an all-depth corner on circle packing and Heilbronn — [arXiv:2605.29268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29268)

**General optimization guidance on cost axes**
- Report FEs and CPU time, and separate expensive-evaluation time from algorithm overhead — [Bartz-Beielstein et al., arXiv:2007.03488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03488)

### Inferences
- **Choosing the cost axis.** In an AFAR-like pipeline, evaluation dominates cost: about 42 × 5 ≈ 210 CPU-h per generation vs the GPU serving job (from the project context). "Equal generations" is a reasonable first-order cost match only if every candidate consumes the full simulation budget. Cascades, compile failures and timeouts break this, so the x-axis should be *measured* consumption (Slurm accounting of CPU-s and GPU-s), not nominal counts.
- **Model levers.** Tokens (Oved et al.) are a good model-agnostic proxy for LLM cost only when the model is fixed. When comparing a 7B against a larger self-hosted model, charge GPU-hours actually reserved, or effective FLOPs (Xing et al.), because per-token cost differs by model size and cache behaviour. Report LLM-side cost (GPU-h, tokens, calls) and evaluation-side cost (CPU-h) separately as well as a combined cost. A combined figure needs an explicit exchange rate, e.g., the cluster's service-unit charge per CPU-h vs GPU-h, and conclusions should be checked under ±2× changes in that rate.
- **Baselines.** Always include a cost-matched "no-evolution" baseline: independent samples from the seed program with the same model and prompt, at equal CPU+GPU cost. The 2024–2026 evidence (Zhang et al.; Oved et al.) shows the margin of evolution over sampling is task-dependent and often small.
- **"Sample efficiency" claims** (ShinkaEvolve's 150 samples, EoH's 2,000 vs FunSearch's 1M) are not normalized for model, prompt length, or evaluation cost. Treat them as anecdotes, not comparisons, unless a run-level distribution and cost axis are given.

- No primary source found reports GPU-hours for a self-hosted LLM evolution campaign alongside evaluation CPU-hours. Most work uses commercial APIs.
- The FunSearch Nature paper could not be read directly (paywall redirect). Its run counts and per-problem sample totals are cited only via EoH, AlphaEvolve and Sim et al.
- AlgoTune's budget definition (reportedly a dollar budget per task) and speedup aggregation were not confirmed from the abstract page.
- ReEvo (NeurIPS 2024) and EvoPrompt (ICLR 2024) cost/seed reporting was not checked in this pass.

## Q3. What ablation designs and metrics do LLM-evolution papers report? Do they report variance across seeds, and how reproducible are they?

### Takeaway
Ablations in flagship systems are small and mostly single-task, with few or no seeds. AlphaEvolve: 2 tasks, 3 seeds, ±SD bands vs "compute budget." ShinkaEvolve: circle packing only, with seed count and variance unstated. CodeEvolve: 3 runs, mean with best/worst bands, Qwen-only due to budget. EoH: 3 runs. Benchmarking-oriented studies (Zhang et al., 5 runs; Xing et al., 10 seeds; Oved et al., 40 seeds) show large run-to-run variance and frequent early lock-in, which makes best-of-unspecified-runs reporting unreliable. Reproducibility is limited by closed models, unreported budgets and baselines copied from papers rather than re-run.

### Cited Findings
- **AlphaEvolve ablation** (Fig. 8). Variants: no evolution (the same initial program re-fed), no context in prompt, no meta-prompt evolution, no full-file evolution (only the loss evolved), and small base LLM only. Tasks: tensor decomposition for matrix multiplication and kissing-number lower bounds. Curves are averaged over targets; shaded bands are intra-target SD across "three independent runs ... initialized with different random seeds." The x-axis is "increasing compute budget." Conclusion: "each of the components is responsible for a significant improvement in the results" — [AlphaEvolve, arXiv:2506.13131](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13131)
- **ShinkaEvolve ablations** (Fig. 9, circle packing only). Parent selection (best-of-N from the initial program, hill climbing, weighted sampling); LLM ensembling (single GPT-5-nano, fixed uniform ensemble, UCB1-style bandit, the last called "significantly" better); novelty rejection (none, embedding cosine > 0.95, embedding + LLM judge, the judge giving a "marginal" gain). The ablation budget, number of seeds and any variance/CIs are not reported in the text — [ShinkaEvolve, arXiv:2509.19349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19349)
- **CodeEvolve ablations.** Full method vs naive evolution vs no evolution; depth vs inspiration operators; MAP-Elites variants (CVT/Grid/Naive); migration topology (Cycle/Complete/Empty). Each was run three times with Qwen3-Coder-30B; budget "prevented a full-scale ablation study using the Gemini ensemble." Curves show the mean with best/worst shading, the authors arguing "the standard deviation is an unreliable estimator" with three runs — [CodeEvolve v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14150v3). The v6 text adds that best-run metrics "can hide instability and should therefore be reported alongside per-run outcomes, means, and uncertainty," and that CIs from three runs "should not be interpreted as strong evidence of statistical significance" — [CodeEvolve v6](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14150v6)
- **EoH ablations.** Variants E1-only, E1+E2, and code-only (EoC), all sharing an initial population and matched on number of evaluated heuristics, "carried out in the same experimental settings three times." The paper reports per-run values and averages (e.g., average gap: code→code 2.57%, T2T2C 2.13%, T&C2T2C 0.85%, full EoH 0.66%). The main tables report no SD or tests — [EoH, arXiv:2401.02051](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02051)
- **Zhang et al. variance.** 5 runs. Tables give mean ± SD of top-1 and best-of-5. Variance is large in places, e.g., EoH with CodeLlama-34B on admissible set 14.09 ± 5.43 and ReEvo/CodeLlama-7B 10.79 ± 6.16. LLM choice strongly affects results; bigger models "do not necessarily lead to better performance"; "there may not be universally effective and efficient LLM-based EPS method for all AHD problems" — [arXiv:2407.10873](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10873)
- **Xing et al. stagnation.** "Identical configurations produce widely varying final fitness." Case studies attribute this to runs anchoring early on one algorithmic family (e.g., scipy optimization vs hand-coded construction). A capability gate means Llama-3.1-8B never achieves both a significant depth gain and a non-trivial level. Prompt differences across systems confound comparisons: ShinkaEvolve's circle-packing prompt suggests scipy. Bandit allocation across parallel trajectories (UCB, EXP3.P, Thompson) improved mean fitness by 12.3% over the strongest island baseline, and Thompson reached thresholds with about 40% fewer generations than random allocation — [arXiv:2605.29268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29268)
- **Exploits and auditing.** In Oved et al., the top transaction-scheduling program at equal proposals scored 5.06× baseline by placing only 3 of 300 transactions. After re-execution auditing, the ratio fell to about 1.02–1.04 — [arXiv:2610.23977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23977)
- **BLADE use case.** Comparing LLaMEA mutation-prompt strategies inside a standardized logging and analysis pipeline, as a template for prompt-lever ablations (details from an AI-generated summary; verify against the paper) — [BLADE, arXiv:2504.20183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20183)
- **OpenEvolve reproducibility.** "every component is seeded"; README table: "Fully deterministic" — [OpenEvolve README](https://github.com/algorithmicsuperintelligence/openevolve). Default `random_seed: 42` and `max_iterations: 100`; defaults `num_islands: 5`, `migration_interval: 50`, `migration_rate: 0.1`, `exploitation_ratio: 0.7`, `exploration_ratio: 0.2`, `elite_selection_ratio: 0.1` — [default_config.yaml](https://github.com/algorithmicsuperintelligence/openevolve/blob/main/configs/default_config.yaml) (the seed defaults are also present in the installed 0.4.0 `config.py`)
- **LLM inference non-determinism.** Inference servers can return different outputs for identical prompts even at temperature 0, because kernels are not batch-invariant (gist from prior knowledge of the post; specific figures not re-verified) — [Thinking Machines Lab, "Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference" (2025)](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)

### Inferences
- **Ablation design for levers.** Use one-factor-at-a-time from a fixed baseline (model, prompt, population/islands, exploitation ratio, seed program, traces, sim length) with the same seed set across arms. Match arms on measured cost (Q2), not generations. Include a cost-matched sampling arm in every family of ablations. Pre-register the primary metric (e.g., normalized AUC of best-so-far geomean speedup vs CPU+GPU-h) and 2–3 targets. With many levers and few seeds, a screening design (1–2 seeds per arm, many arms) followed by confirmation (≥ 5 seeds on the 2–3 most promising arms) beats spreading 3 seeds evenly.
- **Report what the flagship papers mostly omit**: per-seed curves, run counts, failed/timeout rates, total measured cost, and audited (re-executed, held-out) scores of the selected program.
- **Determinism claims.** "Fully deterministic" holds for OpenEvolve's own sampling, not necessarily for LLM generation served with batching. Paired-seed designs reduce but may not remove LLM-side variance, so pairing should be treated as approximate.

### Gaps
- ShinkaEvolve's appendix (beyond the first 100k characters fetched) might contain seed counts for ablations. Not confirmed.
- AlphaEvolve's ablation curves carry no numeric axis units in the text, so cost-per-improvement cannot be read off.
- No quantitative study found of LLM-serving non-determinism's contribution to run-to-run variance in evolution campaigns.

## Q4. How are noisy or expensive evaluations handled (cascades, surrogates, racing, multi-fidelity), and how should a cheap screening fidelity be judged against an expensive one?

### Takeaway
LLM-evolution systems cut evaluation cost mainly with staged cascades: cheap tests first, the expensive suite only for survivors (AlphaEvolve; OpenEvolve's `evaluate_stage1/2/3` with absolute thresholds). They also avoid near-duplicate evaluations (ShinkaEvolve novelty rejection). Configuration-level budget allocation is the domain of racing and bandit methods (irace, successive halving, Hyperband; bandit allocation across trajectories in BaSE). The standard way to validate a cheap fidelity is rank agreement with the expensive one: Kendall τ or Spearman ρ, ideally restricted to the top of the distribution, top-k overlap or recall, and selection regret. Global rank correlation can overstate a proxy's usefulness for picking winners.

### Cited Findings

**Evaluation cascades**
- AlphaEvolve: "new solutions are evaluated on the next stage only if they achieve sufficiently promising results in all earlier stages." New solutions are first tested "on a small scale before being subjected to the main test cases." Hard-to-code properties such as simplicity "can be graded using separate LLM calls" and added to scores or used to discard programs. AlphaEvolve optimizes multiple scores, and "optimizing multiple metrics often improves results for the single target metric" — [AlphaEvolve, arXiv:2506.13131](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13131)
- OpenEvolve 0.4.0: `cascade_evaluation: true` by default, with default `cascade_thresholds` of [0.5, 0.75, 0.9]. The evaluator module must define `evaluate_stage1` and may define `evaluate_stage2`/`evaluate_stage3`. A stage passes if `combined_score >= threshold`, or, if no `combined_score` exists, if the average of all numeric metrics except `error` does. Other defaults: `timeout: 300` s, `max_retries: 3`, `parallel_evaluations: 4`, `use_llm_feedback: false`, `llm_feedback_weight: 0.1` — [OpenEvolve default_config.yaml](https://github.com/algorithmicsuperintelligence/openevolve/blob/main/configs/default_config.yaml); [OpenEvolve evaluator.py](https://github.com/algorithmicsuperintelligence/openevolve/blob/main/openevolve/evaluator.py); [config.py](https://github.com/algorithmicsuperintelligence/openevolve/blob/main/openevolve/config.py) (verified against the installed openevolve-0.4.0 package: `_passes_threshold` and `cascade_thresholds` default)
- ShinkaEvolve has no staged cascade. It rejects proposals whose code embedding has cosine similarity > 0.95 to an archive member, optionally confirmed by an LLM novelty judge, with up to 3 patch resamples (10 for MoE). AIME candidates are evaluated over 3 runs to average noise — [ShinkaEvolve, arXiv:2509.19349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19349)

**Noise**
- Real-world noise "can be non-normal, time-varying, and even be dependent on system states," and simple repetition is insufficient. Bokhari et al. (2020) compare five noise-handling approaches — [Bartz-Beielstein et al., arXiv:2007.03488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03488)

**Racing and bandit budget allocation**
- irace (iterated racing): samples candidate configurations, races them on instances while eliminating statistically worse ones (Friedman/t-tests), then resamples around elites until the budget is spent — [López-Ibáñez et al. 2016, Oper. Res. Perspect. 3:43–58, DOI 10.1016/j.orp.2016.09.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.orp.2016.09.002)
- Successive halving: give n configurations a small budget, keep the top fraction, and multiply the survivors' budget — [Jamieson & Talwalkar, AISTATS 2016, arXiv:1502.07943](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.07943). Hyperband hedges over the n-vs-budget-per-configuration trade-off by running several successive-halving brackets — [Li et al., JMLR 18(185) 2018](https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/16-558.html). Secondary descriptions note that successive halving combines multi-fidelity evaluation with racing — [Analyzing the Impact of Undersampling on Benchmarking and Configuration of EAs, arXiv:2204.09353](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09353)
- Sim et al. used irace to tune LLM-evolved bin-packing heuristics, changing average excess bins by −2.50% (FS1), +0.63% (FS2), +0.88% (FSW) and −1.01% (EoH) — [arXiv:2501.11411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11411)
- Within LLM evolution, BaSE allocates LLM calls across K parallel trajectories with UCB/EXP3.P/Thompson bandits — [Xing et al., arXiv:2605.29268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29268)

**Judging a cheap fidelity against an expensive one**
- NAS evaluation surveys use Kendall τ (pairwise concordance), N@K (best true rank among the predicted top-K), and Precision@K (overlap of predicted and true top-K) to assess cheap performance estimators — [Efficient Evaluation Methods for NAS: A Survey, arXiv:2301.05919](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05919)
- Kendall τ is generally more robust to errors than Spearman ρ, and can be computed on the top 5% of the space rather than all architectures (snippet-level) — [Zero-Shot NAS survey, arXiv:2307.01998](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01998)
- Global Kendall τ can overstate proxy quality. A "sparse Kendall-Tau" focusing on top candidates tracks stand-alone performance better — [Yu et al., "How to Train Your Super-Net," arXiv:2003.04276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04276)
- Example validation: a tuned cheap training scheme vs the reference scheme reached Kendall τ = 0.926 across 120 held-out models — [Accel-NASBench, arXiv:2404.08005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08005)
- Low-fidelity estimates can distort rankings, especially for large or complex candidates — [Survey on computationally efficient NAS, arXiv:2206.01520](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01520) (snippet-level)

### Inferences
- **Cascade semantics.** OpenEvolve's cascade thresholds are absolute values on `combined_score` (default 0.5/0.75/0.9), not relative to the parent or a baseline. If fitness is a geomean speedup near 1.0, the defaults would pass almost any compiling candidate. Thresholds should be set relative to LRU (1.0) or to the current population (e.g., stage 1 must exceed a fraction of the parent's score), or stage scores rescaled. Check what `combined_score` the evaluator actually emits.
- **Candidate fidelities for a ChampSim-style evaluator**: fewer traces (e.g., 3–4 of 14), fewer simulated instructions or shorter warmup, and compile-plus-smoke-test. A deterministic simulator has little run-to-run noise per (trace, length). The "noise" of a cheap fidelity is mostly approximation bias from trace subset and length, so repeated evaluation does not help but better fidelity design does.
- **Calibration protocol, using existing campaign logs at no new compute.** Take N ≈ 50–200 already fully evaluated candidates spanning the fitness range, re-score them at the cheap fidelity, and report:
  1. Spearman ρ and Kendall τ overall and on the top quartile (sparse-τ style).
  2. Top-k recall: the share of the full-fidelity top-k that survives the cheap screen at the planned pass rate.
  3. Selection regret: full-fidelity fitness of the true best minus that of the cheap-fidelity pick.
  4. False-rejection rate of candidates that would have beaten the parent at full fidelity.

  Accept a fidelity only if top-k recall and regret are acceptable *at the pass rate the cascade will use*.
- **Racing at the lever level.** Successive halving or irace logic can allocate campaign seeds: start many lever settings with short or 1-seed campaigns, then promote the best fraction to longer or more-seeded campaigns. The fidelity here is campaign length, so its rank agreement (early best-so-far vs final) should be checked the same way, since anytime curves can cross.

### Gaps
- No LLM-evolution paper found reports rank-correlation or top-k validation of its own cascade stages against the full evaluation. Cascade thresholds appear to be set by hand.
- Surrogate models predicting program fitness from code (e.g., embedding regressors) in LLM evolution were not found in this pass.
- Bokhari et al. (2020) on noise handling was not read directly.

## Q5. How is generalization or overfitting of evolved programs measured?

### Takeaway
Held-out evaluation is standard in applied deployments (AlphaEvolve's Borg heuristic on "an unseen test dataset"; kernel tiling with half the shapes held out; ShinkaEvolve's ALE-Bench public→private and AIME 2024→2023/2025). It is absent for pure math constructions and missing from several benchmark studies. Independent re-evaluation shows that LLM-evolved bin-packing heuristics trained on narrow distributions often generalize poorly; simple Best-Fit is the best generalist across 6,064 instances. GP research offers mitigation via training-subset sampling: random sampling and interleaved sampling of training cases reduce overfitting and bloat.

### Cited Findings
- **AlphaEvolve.** The Borg scheduling heuristic was measured "on an unseen test dataset of recent workloads and capacity to ensure generalization" via a simulator, and post-deployment results "confirmed the simulator results." For Gemini kernel tiling, "half of these input shapes form the training set" and the "remaining input shapes form the evaluation set." No held-out split is described for the mathematical problems — [AlphaEvolve, arXiv:2506.13131](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13131)
- **EoH train/test design.**
  - Bin packing: train on five Weibull 5k-item instances (capacity 100); test on 1k/5k/10k items × capacity 100/500, five instances per set.
  - TSP: train on 64 TSP100; test on TSPLib and 1,000 random instances each at n = 20/50/100.
  - FSSP: train on 64 random 50-job instances; test on Taillard sets.

  Generalization is reported across sizes and capacities. The out-of-distribution claim vs neural solvers is qualitative — [EoH, arXiv:2401.02051](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02051)
- **Zhang et al.** describe no held-out set. Training instances (20 OR, 5 Weibull, 64 TSP100) guide the search directly — [arXiv:2407.10873](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10873)
- **Sim, Renau & Hart, "Beyond the Hype" (Jan 2025).** Five LLM-evolved heuristics (FunSearch FS1/FS2 trained on OR1-style instances, FSW on Weibull; EoH and EoC on Weibull) were evaluated against Next/First/Best/Worst/Almost-Worst-Fit on 12 datasets with 6,064 instances, excluding the training-like OR sets. Results:
  - Best-Fit ranked first, "an excellent generalist heuristic."
  - FSW was worst on every dataset, with average excess bins 113.21 vs 5.85 for Best-Fit.
  - EoH never won any dataset.
  - FS2 ranked 8th on independent datasets but 1st on evolved single-distribution datasets.
  - Conclusion: "most of the LLM heuristics do not generalise well when evaluated across a broad range of benchmarks."
  - Recommendations: diverse datasets, instance-space analysis, simple baselines, and weighing specialist gains against generation cost.

  — [arXiv:2501.11411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11411)
- A later study reports that baseline heuristics generalize to other distributions (e.g., Uniform(30,100), Weibull(7.0,75)) while some evolved heuristics do not, "confirming findings by Sim et al." (search snippet only) — [An In-depth Study of LLM Contributions to the Bin Packing Problem, arXiv:2510.27353](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27353)
- **ShinkaEvolve.**
  - ALE-Bench: the best program was selected on public tests and scored on private tests; the top-5 public solutions averaged 1923.5 → 1927.0 private, which the authors read as no significant overfitting.
  - AIME scaffold: evolved on AIME 2024, tested on 2023 (smaller gains, possibly contaminated) and 2025, and transferred to other models.
  - MoE loss: evolved at 556M parameters and evaluated at 2.7B.
  - Circle packing used relaxed verification (1e-6 slack): relaxed score 2.635983 vs 2.635978 under exact re-verification.

  — [ShinkaEvolve, arXiv:2509.19349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19349)
- **Evaluator exploits as a form of overfitting to the evaluator.** A 5.06× "improvement" from placing 3 of 300 transactions collapsed to about 1.02× after auditing. The authors recommend re-execution and coverage checks — [Oved et al., arXiv:2610.23977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23977)
- **BLADE** explicitly targets generalisation vs specialisation capabilities, using instance generators and held-out instances on MA-BBOB/SBOX-COST — [BLADE, arXiv:2504.20183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20183)
- **GP overfitting control (Gonçalves, Silva et al.).**
  - The Random Sampling Technique (RST) evaluates each generation on a random subset of training data. It reduced overfitting on all three symbolic-regression datasets, improved test fitness on two, and produced smaller solutions — [Gonçalves et al., EuroGP 2012](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gp-html/goncalves_2012_EuroGP.html)
  - RST settings maximizing variation between generations significantly reduced overfitting on a toy problem — [Gonçalves & Silva, EPIA 2011](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gp-html/goncalves2011experiments.html)
  - Interleaved sampling alternates a single training case with the full training set — [Gonçalves & Silva, EuroGP 2013](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gp-html/goncalves_2013_EuroGP.html)

### Inferences
- **Three-way trace split.** (a) A training set of traces used for in-loop fitness. (b) A validation set used only to choose which evolved program(s) to report or deploy. Choosing the best of thousands of candidates on the training fitness carries a winner's-curse optimism, exactly the public/private gap ALE-Bench measures. (c) A held-out test set from different workload families, simulated at full length, for the final number. Report the train→test gap for the selected program and for the baseline policies (LRU and the published policies), not only for the winner.
- **Comparing levers on generalization.** "Searches better" should be judged on held-out geomean speedup of the program each campaign would select (via its validation set), not on training-fitness best-so-far alone. A lever that raises training fitness but widens the train/test gap may just be overfitting the 14 traces.
- **RST-style trace subsampling.** Random trace subsets per generation could both reduce overfitting and cut CPU cost, but fitness values become comparable only within the same subset. The archive would need re-evaluation on a common set, or subset-aware selection, to avoid noisy elitism. This is an inference from the GP literature, untested in LLM evolution in the sources found.
- **Audit selected programs.** Re-simulate them from scratch, check for evaluator exploits (e.g., policies that crash, skip, or game warmup and stat-collection boundaries), and log coverage-like sanity metrics during the run (Oved et al.).

### Gaps
- FunSearch's own bin-packing train/held-out protocol could not be read directly. A search snippet says the heuristic was trained on 5 OR-Library instances and evaluated on the other 20, but which paper says this was not confirmed.
- No source found that measures overfitting of evolved *cache replacement* or other microarchitectural policies to training traces. This domain-specific evidence is missing (and was out of scope for project-specific research).
- The McDermott/White critiques' specific recommendations on train/test splits for GP benchmarks were not verified in full text.
